# Research and editorial notes

Prepared 4 October 2026. Source IDs match the Notes sections in SCRIPT.md. Dialogue, characters and comic situations are original fiction. Analogies explain ideas; they are not experimental findings. Practical conversation suggestions are editorial applications, not interventions tested by the cited studies.

## S1 — Emotional expression and social expectations (scene 2)

[APA: A closer look at the guidelines for boys and men](https://www.apa.org/news/apa/2019/boys-men-look.html).

Professional guidance describes how expectations about masculinity can discourage vulnerability and seeking help. The script says some boys learn these expectations. It does not claim all men hide emotion, that men feel more than women, or that quietness establishes a diagnosis.

## S2 — Fathers and caregiving experience (scene 3)

[Abraham and colleagues, 2014: Father's brain is sensitive to childcare experiences, PNAS](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1402569111).

Brain imaging compared groups of parents with different caregiving roles. Fathers' involvement was associated with connectivity between parenting-related networks. This was not a randomized caregiving-training experiment; association does not establish causation. It supports challenging biological exclusivity, not claiming identical parenting or eliminating individual differences.

## S3 — Daily emotional variability (scene 4)

[Little evidence for sex or ovarian hormone influences on affective variability, Scientific Reports, 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8536752/).

Researchers used 75 daily emotion assessments in adult men, naturally cycling women and women using different oral contraceptives. Findings challenged assumptions of large group differences in emotional variability. This is one study, not proof that hormones never affect emotion; it does not establish identical emotional lives across all ages or populations.

## S4 — PMS and PMDD qualification (scene 4)

[NHS: PMS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/pre-menstrual-syndrome/) and [NHS: PMDD](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/pmdd-premenstrual-dysphoric-disorder/).

Cycle-related symptoms can be disruptive, and PMDD can involve severe emotional and physical symptoms. Do not turn the stereotype correction into denial of these conditions. Nor should disagreement be treated as evidence of either condition. If expanding this topic later, include assessment for symptoms that affect daily life.

## S5 — Cognitive similarities and mathematics (scene 5)

[Hyde, 2005: The gender similarities hypothesis](https://www.apa.org/pubs/journals/releases/amp-606581.pdf).

The review found similarity on most, not all, psychological variables. Size and direction of differences depend on the measure, age and context. It does not show that every brain or ability is identical.

[Math-specific corroboration: Lindberg and colleagues, 2010, New trends in gender and mathematics performance](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21038941/).

This meta-analysis supports broadly similar mathematics performance. Group averages cannot decide an individual child's ability or justify assigning tasks by gender. The script encourages asking about skills and interests rather than assuming them.

## S6 — Sleep (scene 7)

[CDC: About sleep](https://www.cdc.gov/sleep/about/).

Sleep supports attention, memory and emotional well-being. Script targets cover ages 6–12, adolescents and adults 18–60. CDC lists 13–17 as its teen category; pediatric sleep consensus includes age 18 in the 8–10-hour range. Older adult targets differ. The maintenance shift is an analogy, not a claim about a literal repair process. Persistent sleep difficulties may need assessment.

## S7 — Physical activity (scene 8)

[CDC: Benefits of physical activity](https://www.cdc.gov/physical-activity-basics/benefits/).

Activity benefits brain health, sleep and physical health. The examples are starting points, not a complete activity prescription; needs and safe options vary with age and ability. Do not say movement cures depression or that one brief walk replaces recommended activity. No exercise demonstration is required in this video.

## S8 — Tooth decay (scene 9)

[NIDCR: The tooth decay process](https://www.nidcr.nih.gov/health-info/tooth-decay/more-info/tooth-decay-process).

Bacterial acids cause mineral loss from enamel. Saliva and fluoride support remineralization before cavitation; an established cavity needs dental care. Twice-daily fluoride toothpaste and reducing frequent sugar exposure are supported. Children need supervision and the appropriate toothpaste amount for their age. Avoid interpreting the acid-factory joke as saying every oral bacterium is harmful.

## S9 — Hearing (scene 10)

[WHO: Safe listening](https://www.who.int/news-room/questions-and-answers/item/deafness-and-hearing-loss-safe-listening).

Risk depends on sound level, exposure duration and repetition. Loud sound can cause irreversible hearing damage. Volume reduction, quiet breaks, exposure monitoring and protection at loud events are sensible measures. Noise cancellation may reduce the urge to overpower background noise; it does not make loud playback safe. Avoid a universal percentage-volume safety promise across devices.

## Editorial decisions

- Two topics teach girls/women about boys/men; two teach boys/men about girls/women. They are corrections of assumptions, not rules about every individual.
- Characters sometimes voice a misconception so another character can correct it immediately. Do not clip only the misconception for promotional material.
- Health habits support well-being; none is presented as a guaranteed treatment or replacement for medical care.
- Jokes target faulty assumptions and everyday situations, not people seeking help or experiencing medical symptoms.
- No unsupported percentages, hormone tests, supplements, diagnosis prompts or paid products.
