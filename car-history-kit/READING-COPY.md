# From Steam to Software — reading copy

## 1. The horse has been made redundant

**Milo:** A car is a sofa with wheels, a controlled fire, and a monthly payment. Somehow we decided children should grow up wanting one.

**Nova:** And the electric version replaces the fire with chemistry, magnets, and arguments about charging.

**Milo:** How did we get from a horse pulling a box to a box that tells us our phone needs updating?

**Nova:** By solving one problem at a time, then discovering three new ones. Today we follow the history, open up the machinery, and explain what actually makes cars move, turn and stop.

**Milo:** Finally, a history lesson with torque.

## 2. 1769: A kettle applies for a driving licence

**Mira:** In seventeen sixty-nine, Nicolas-Joseph Cugnot tested a steam-powered vehicle intended for military hauling. A larger version followed. Early cars did not begin with petrol.

**Milo:** They began with someone looking at a kettle and asking, can this tow artillery?

**Mira:** Fuel heats water in a boiler. Steam pressure pushes pistons. Mechanical linkages turn that back-and-forth movement into wheel motion.

**Milo:** Tea preparation, with consequences.

**Mira:** But boilers were heavy, and starting involved raising steam. Keeping water and fire under control added work. Replacing the horse did not automatically make travel convenient.

**Milo:** The horse could at least start without preheating. Although its emissions were also noticeable.

## 3. 1876–1886: Bring the fire indoors

**Mira:** Internal combustion moved the burning into the engine itself. Instead of making steam elsewhere, burning fuel and air creates hot gases that push a piston directly.

**Nova:** The fire has moved from an external contractor to a permanent member of staff.

**Mira:** In eighteen seventy-six, Nicolaus Otto developed a practical four-stroke compressed-charge engine. In eighteen eighty-six, Carl Benz applied for the patent associated with his purpose-built petrol motor car.

**Nova:** Three wheels, one cylinder, and absolutely no argument about where to put the touchscreen.

**Mira:** Benz is a key milestone, not the sole inventor of everything automotive. Steam vehicles preceded him; Gottlieb Daimler and Wilhelm Maybach were also developing lightweight engines and motor vehicles.

**Nova:** History has a whole workshop. The thumbnail only has room for one moustache.

## 4. 1888: Bertha takes the prototype outside

**Ellie:** In eighteen eighty-eight, Bertha Benz took a Benz motor car on a journey of more than a hundred kilometres, with her two sons.

**Malik:** An ambitious family outing. Especially when roadside assistance is just whichever son looks less tired.

**Ellie:** She travelled from Mannheim to Pforzheim and obtained fuel from a pharmacy along the way. This helped demonstrate that the invention could make a substantial journey.

**Malik:** One bottle of cough syrup, please. And a tank of whatever the carriage is drinking.

**Ellie:** That is our joke, not a historical quotation. The important point is that a working machine also needs fuel supply, repairs and public confidence.

**Malik:** The road test mattered as much as the drawing board. Literally taking the idea for a spin.

## 5. Under the bonnet: The four-stroke dance

**Milo:** Explain the petrol engine without saying, the engine engines.

**Mira:** First: intake. The piston moves down and the intake valve opens, letting air in. Depending on the design, fuel mixes with that air before entering or is injected directly into the cylinder.

**Mira:** Second: compression. The piston moves up with the valves closed, squeezing the charge into a smaller space.

**Milo:** Like packing a suitcase, except the suitcase is about to become extremely enthusiastic.

**Mira:** Third: power. A timed spark starts combustion. The hot gases expand and push the piston down.

**Mira:** Fourth: exhaust. The exhaust valve opens and the rising piston pushes spent gases out. Then the sequence repeats.

**Milo:** Breathe in, squeeze, push, breathe out. The engine has a more consistent workout routine than I do.

**Mira:** Four piston strokes take two crankshaft revolutions. It is controlled combustion; destructive knocking is not the goal.

## 6. Pistons, crankshafts and the engine orchestra

**Jun:** The piston goes up and down. The wheels go round. Who translates?

**Mira:** A connecting rod joins the piston to an offset crank on the crankshaft. That mechanism converts reciprocating motion into rotation. A flywheel helps smooth the pulses.

**Jun:** A translator fluent in both boing and spin.

**Mira:** The camshaft opens valves in step with the crankshaft, commonly through a belt or chain drive. A typical four-stroke camshaft turns once for every two crankshaft turns.

**Mira:** Multiple cylinders deliver their power strokes at different times. Displacement is their combined swept volume, not a direct horsepower score. Breathing, speed, combustion and boost also matter.

**Jun:** So a two-litre engine is a volume measurement, not how much petrol it drinks with lunch.

## 7. Air, fuel and a very punctual spark

**Anaya:** Does pressing the accelerator pour more petrol into the fire?

**Mira:** In a conventional petrol engine, the throttle helps control incoming air. Fuel delivery must match. Older carburettors use pressure differences in flowing air to draw and mix fuel.

**Mira:** Modern injection meters fuel more precisely. Port injection sprays before the intake valve; direct injection sprays into the cylinder. An ignition coil supplies the high voltage for the spark.

**Anaya:** A carburettor is a mechanical recipe. Injection adds a very fussy digital chef.

**Mira:** Too much or too little fuel changes combustion. Timing matters too: ignition is arranged so the pressure rise does useful work as the piston moves.

**Anaya:** The engine wants dinner and an appointment, not just a bucket of enthusiasm.

## 8. Starting and charging: Two different electrical jobs

**Ellie:** If an engine must turn to run, what turns it before it is running?

**Mira:** In a conventional petrol car, the battery powers a starter motor. It turns the engine until combustion can sustain operation; then the starter disengages.

**Ellie:** The engine needs someone to get it out of bed. Relatable.

**Mira:** Once running, the engine commonly drives an alternator. It supplies electrical power and replenishes the small vehicle battery through a regulated charging system.

**Mira:** That is not the same as an electric car’s large traction battery. Some hybrids also use different starter-generator arrangements.

**Ellie:** Starter gets the party going. Alternator keeps the lights on. Neither is powered by the confidence of the person turning the key.

## 9. Oil and coolant: The unglamorous heroes

**Rowan:** If burning fuel makes motion, why not just make everything hotter?

**Mira:** Because engines have temperature limits. In a liquid-cooled design, coolant carries heat from the engine to a radiator, where it passes to the surrounding air. A thermostat helps regulate circulation.

**Rowan:** The radiator is the engine politely opening a window.

**Mira:** Oil does a different job: it forms lubricating films between moving surfaces, reduces wear and also helps carry heat and contamination. Coolant and oil are not interchangeable beverages.

**Rowan:** And the metal rings around the piston?

**Mira:** They help seal combustion pressure and control oil on the cylinder wall. The whole machine depends on clearances, seals and lubrication, not just dramatic flames.

**Rowan:** So the engine is mostly tiny gaps being managed extremely seriously.

## 10. The gearbox: A bicycle lesson with invoices

**Milo:** Why does a running engine not stall whenever the car stops?

**Mira:** A manual car can disconnect the engine from the gearbox using its clutch. Reconnecting progressively lets the engine bring the car into motion.

**Mira:** Gears trade rotational speed for torque, which means turning force. A low gear multiplies wheel torque while the wheels turn more slowly relative to the engine. Think of climbing on a bicycle.

**Milo:** The easier pedalling gear. My legs understand engineering when there is a hill.

**Mira:** Higher gears suit faster travel with fewer engine revolutions per wheel turn. Gears do not create free power; real transmissions also lose some energy as heat.

**Milo:** And horsepower?

**Mira:** Power is how quickly work is done. For a rotating shaft, it depends on torque and rotational speed together. A large torque number alone does not describe the whole performance.

## 11. The differential: Why corners require diplomacy

**Nova:** Both driven wheels get power. Why not bolt them to one solid shaft?

**Mira:** During a turn, the outside wheel travels farther than the inside wheel. A differential transmits torque while allowing different wheel speeds.

**Nova:** Like two friends walking around a roundabout. The outside friend needs more steps and probably more snacks.

**Mira:** An ordinary open differential has a traction limitation: if one wheel can support very little torque, that can limit useful torque at the other. Limited-slip and locking designs address this differently.

**Nova:** And steering?

**Mira:** A common rack-and-pinion system converts steering-wheel rotation into sideways rack movement that turns the road wheels. Hydraulic or electric assistance reduces driver effort.

**Nova:** Power steering: because parking should not be an upper-body qualification exam.

## 12. 1897: Diesel does ignition differently

**Mira:** Meanwhile, another engine branch developed. Rudolf Diesel and the Augsburg engineering works produced a successful diesel engine in eighteen ninety-seven.

**Malik:** Same piston, different motivational technique?

**Mira:** A conventional diesel compresses air strongly enough to heat it. Fuel is then injected into that hot compressed air and ignites without a spark plug initiating the normal combustion event.

**Malik:** The petrol engine needs a spark. The diesel engine brings its own heated discussion.

**Mira:** Glow plugs can help cold starting; they are not spark plugs. Diesel designs can be efficient, but soot and nitrogen oxides require attention. Diesel development did not instantly replace petrol cars.

**Malik:** Two branches of the family tree. Both capable of expensive noises.

## 13. Around 1900: Three engines enter the chat

**Ellie:** Around nineteen hundred, the road was not a petrol-only club. Steam, battery-electric and petrol vehicles competed.

**Jun:** Electric cars were not invented when phones started losing headphone sockets?

**Ellie:** No. Electric vehicles offered quiet operation and avoided hand-cranking. Steam had its own strengths, but heating up and managing water could be inconvenient. Batteries limited range and charging opportunities.

**Jun:** So the argument about how to power a car is older than most car companies.

**Ellie:** Petrol cars gained advantages from falling prices, refuelling infrastructure and improved convenience. The electric starter, introduced on Cadillac in nineteen twelve, helped remove the hand-cranking ordeal.

**Jun:** Humanity invented an electric motor so it could more conveniently start its non-electric motor. Beautifully complicated.

## 14. 1908–1913: The invention becomes a production problem

**Mira:** Ford introduced the Model T in nineteen-oh-eight. In nineteen thirteen, its team developed moving assembly-line production at Highland Park. Ford did not invent the car.

**Milo:** He helped invent the situation where everybody could eventually complain about traffic.

**Mira:** Instead of workers repeatedly travelling around a stationary vehicle, work came past organised stations. Standardised parts and specialised tasks improved throughput.

**Mira:** The Library of Congress describes assembly time falling from twelve and a half hours to ninety-three minutes. That is seven hundred and fifty minutes versus ninety-three on our chart.

**Milo:** The car was not driving faster. The factory was.

**Mira:** This compares reported assembly time, not the time to mine steel and manufacture every component. Lower costs expanded access, though affordability was never universal.

## 15. Bodies, layouts and the disappearing third pedal

**Mira:** Through the early twentieth century, cars became more integrated machines. Many used bodies mounted on separate frames; unitised structures instead make much of the body carry structural loads.

**Anaya:** The difference between putting a house on scaffolding and designing the house to support itself.

**Mira:** Engine placement and driven wheels varied. Front-wheel drive groups propulsion near the front; rear-wheel drive sends power rearward. Packaging, weight distribution and cost all influence the choice.

**Mira:** For the nineteen-forty model year, Oldsmobile offered the Hydra-Matic automatic. Later automatic designs often used torque converters; that original Hydra-Matic used a fluid coupling.

**Anaya:** Important distinction. History also needs its fluids checked.

**Mira:** Automatic gear changes reduced the driver's workload. Today there are several approaches, including planetary automatics, dual-clutch systems and continuously variable transmissions.

## 16. Automatic, dual-clutch and CVT: Three ways to shift

**Jun:** Automatic is one gearbox design, right?

**Mira:** It describes who handles the shifting, not one universal mechanism. A common automatic uses a torque converter to transfer engine power through moving fluid, with gearsets and clutches providing different ratios.

**Jun:** A fluid handshake instead of my left foot negotiating with a clutch pedal.

**Mira:** A dual-clutch transmission divides gears between two clutches, commonly odd and even sets, so the next gear can be prepared before the handover.

**Mira:** A belt or chain CVT changes its effective pulley diameters to vary the ratio continuously. It need not jump between a fixed set of steps.

**Jun:** Stairs versus a ramp. Both get you upstairs, with different complaints from enthusiasts.

**Mira:** And an e-CVT label on some hybrids can refer to a different power-split system, not that belt-and-pulley design. Similar names do not guarantee identical machinery.

## 17. Tyres and suspension: Negotiating with the road

**Rowan:** Could engineers solve bad roads by making the seats extremely fluffy?

**Mira:** Comfort matters, but so does keeping tyres in contact with the road. Springs support the vehicle and let wheels move over bumps. Dampers control the resulting bouncing.

**Rowan:** Springs are the enthusiastic friend. Dampers are the friend saying, please calm down.

**Mira:** Tyres provide the grip for accelerating, steering and braking. Michelin filed its radial-tyre patent in nineteen forty-six, an important step in tyre construction.

**Mira:** In a radial design, casing cords run across the tyre from bead to bead, with belts supporting the tread. Construction, rubber, pressure and road conditions all affect behaviour.

**Rowan:** A powerful engine still negotiates with the road through rubber. Expensive horsepower, meet wet roundabout.

## 18. Brakes: Turning urgency into heat

**Jun:** The brakes destroy speed. Where does the speed go?

**Mira:** Friction brakes convert much of the car's kinetic energy into heat. In a common hydraulic system, pedal force creates fluid pressure that operates brakes at the wheels.

**Mira:** Disc brakes clamp pads against a rotating disc. Drum brakes press shoes against the inside of a drum. The tyres must still grip the road to slow the car.

**Jun:** So the car warms its dinner plates to prevent us becoming a collision.

**Mira:** At the same mass, kinetic energy depends on speed squared. At thirty kilometres per hour, call it one unit. At sixty, it is four units. At ninety, nine.

**Jun:** Double the speed, quadruple the energy. Physics charges surge pricing.

**Mira:** This is an energy comparison, not a measured stopping-distance chart. Reaction time, grip, gradient and the braking system also matter.

## 19. 1959: The passenger is part of the engineering

**Nova:** Early advertising concentrated on going. When did we start worrying about surviving the stopping?

**Mira:** Safety developed through many contributions. In nineteen fifty-nine, Volvo introduced Nils Bohlin's modern three-point belt. It restrains the torso and pelvis together.

**Nova:** A diagonal strap doing more useful work than most motivational slogans.

**Mira:** A crumple zone absorbs energy through controlled deformation while the passenger compartment aims to preserve survival space. Restraints help manage the occupant's deceleration.

**Mira:** Airbags supplement seat belts; they do not replace them. Simply making every part of a car rigid would not make the people inside invulnerable.

**Nova:** The goal is not a car that wins an argument with a wall. It is occupants who get another day.

## 20. 1967 onward: The engine gets a nervous system

**Mira:** Bosch put its Jetronic electronic petrol injection into series production in nineteen sixty-seven. Electronics increasingly helped control what had been a largely mechanical balancing act.

**Malik:** The engine got a brain. Mine still forgets why it entered the kitchen.

**Mira:** Sensors measure conditions such as airflow or pressure, temperature and crankshaft position. A controller uses this information to command fuel delivery and ignition.

**Mira:** Oxygen sensing in the exhaust can provide feedback for mixture control. Electronic accelerator pedals send a request to the controller rather than always tugging a cable directly.

**Malik:** The accelerator is now an email marked urgent.

**Mira:** Precise control can improve starting, drivability, efficiency and emissions. The trade-off is more components and software to understand when something goes wrong.

## 21. 1970s: The exhaust becomes everyone’s business

**Ellie:** The twentieth century made cars common. It also made breathing beside a busy road a questionable hobby.

**Mira:** Oil shocks and air-quality concerns increased pressure for economy and cleaner exhaust. In the United States, catalytic converters became common on new cars from the nineteen-seventy-five model year.

**Mira:** A catalyst helps chemical reactions occur. Modern three-way converters tackle carbon monoxide, unburned hydrocarbons and nitrogen oxides when operating under the right conditions.

**Ellie:** An exhaust cleanup crew. Not a magical pipe that turns every bad thing into butterflies.

**Mira:** Correct. It does not eliminate carbon dioxide. Pollution controls, efficiency improvements and fuel changes addressed different problems, and countries adopted them at different times.

**Ellie:** Cleaner exhaust and zero environmental impact are very different claims. One fits a laboratory. The other fits an advertisement.

## 22. The turbo: Your exhaust gets a second job

**Milo:** More power means we simply make the engine bigger?

**Mira:** Not necessarily. A turbocharger uses energy in the exhaust flow to spin a turbine. A shaft connects it to a compressor that pushes more air into the engine.

**Milo:** The exhaust was leaving work. Management has found it another task.

**Mira:** More air allows more fuel to burn when the engine is designed and controlled for it. An intercooler reduces the temperature of the compressed intake air.

**Mira:** Boost control limits pressure. The system adds heat and mechanical demands, and some combinations have a delay in response called turbo lag. It is not free energy.

**Milo:** A supercharger?

**Mira:** A conventional supercharger is driven mechanically by the engine rather than by an exhaust turbine. Similar aim, different way of paying the energy bill.

## 23. 1983 and beyond: There is more than one national story

**Anaya:** So car history is Germany inventing, America mass-producing, then everyone else buying?

**Jun:** That leaves out a very large part of the map. Production, design and manufacturing methods developed across many countries, including major European and Japanese industries.

**Anaya:** In India, the first Maruti eight hundred reached its first customer in December nineteen eighty-three. The company began as a government and Suzuki joint venture.

**Jun:** A significant chapter in Indian motoring, not the first car ever seen in India.

**Anaya:** Exactly. Different markets needed different combinations of price, space, fuel economy and serviceability. Progress was not simply making every car larger.

**Jun:** Sometimes progress was fitting the family budget into a smaller parking space. The relatives still requested more legroom.

## 24. 1978–1995: Brakes learn to think in milliseconds

**Mira:** Another strand matured alongside those changes: electronic safety. Bosch's production-ready electronic anti-lock braking system arrived in nineteen seventy-eight; electronic stability control followed in nineteen ninety-five with Mercedes-Benz.

**Rowan:** We finally gave the brakes permission to interrupt bad decisions.

**Mira:** Anti-lock braking uses wheel-speed information to modulate pressure when wheels approach locking. Keeping wheels rolling helps retain steering control during hard braking.

**Mira:** Stability control compares the driver's intended direction with the vehicle's motion. It can brake individual wheels and reduce engine torque to counter a developing skid.

**Rowan:** An invisible referee for the car's dance moves.

**Mira:** But tyres still have limited grip. Electronics cannot cancel ice, excessive speed or the laws of physics.

## 25. 1997: The hybrid stops choosing sides

**Malik:** Petrol versus electric. Can the car simply refuse to pick a team?

**Mira:** That is one reason hybrids are interesting. The Toyota Prius launched in Japan in nineteen ninety-seven, a landmark mass-produced hybrid passenger car. Hybrid experiments existed much earlier.

**Mira:** A hybrid combines a combustion engine, electric motor and battery. Depending on the design, the engine can drive the wheels, generate electricity, or do both through the powertrain.

**Malik:** A group project where the participants actually have different useful skills.

**Mira:** A conventional hybrid does not plug into the grid. Its stored electrical energy comes from the engine and recovered braking energy. A plug-in hybrid can also charge externally.

**Malik:** So self-charging does not mean the car discovered a loophole in thermodynamics.

## 26. Regeneration: A refund, not free money

**Ellie:** How does slowing down charge a battery?

**Mira:** The drivetrain can make an electric motor act as a generator. Taking energy from the moving car creates a resisting torque, while some of that energy returns electrically to the battery.

**Ellie:** So friction braking turns the receipt into heat. Regeneration gets part of the purchase refunded.

**Mira:** Exactly: part. Conversion losses remain. Battery temperature, charge level and power limits can restrict recovery. Ordinary friction brakes still have important work to do.

**Ellie:** Could I accelerate and brake repeatedly to make extra electricity?

**Mira:** No. Every round trip loses energy. Regeneration recovers some energy you already spent; it does not manufacture a bonus supply.

**Ellie:** Physics has rejected my cashback scheme.

## 27. 2008–2010: Electric cars make a louder quiet entrance

**Nova:** Battery-electric cars returned to wider attention as batteries, power electronics and manufacturing developed. This was a comeback, not a brand-new invention.

**Jun:** The early electric car had been waiting for a very long software update.

**Nova:** The original Tesla Roadster entered production in two thousand eight. The Nissan Leaf followed in two thousand ten as a major attempt to bring modern battery-electric motoring to a wider market.

**Jun:** One made electric performance conspicuous. The other looked more ready for groceries.

**Nova:** Neither company invented the electric motor or the electric car. These were milestones in a much longer story involving many firms and researchers.

**Jun:** And the horse, observing all this from a field, remained comfortably unsubscribed.

## 28. Inside an EV: Magnets replace the combustion party

**Mira:** In a typical modern battery-electric drivetrain, the battery supplies direct current. An inverter controls electrical power for the traction motor.

**Mira:** Controlled currents in the stationary part, the stator, create magnetic fields. Their interaction with the rotor produces turning force. Motor designs differ in how they create those magnetic fields.

**Milo:** A carefully timed magnetic dance, instead of pistons having a heated disagreement.

**Mira:** Many electric cars use a single fixed reduction ratio because their motors work across a broad speed range. They still have gears; some designs use more than one ratio.

**Milo:** Fewer gear changes does not mean no engineering.

**Mira:** Right. Bearings, reduction gears, a differential, cooling and control electronics still matter. A small auxiliary electrical system also powers accessories and control functions.

## 29. Inside the battery: Chemistry, not bottled lightning

**Anaya:** What actually travels through a lithium-ion battery?

**Mira:** During discharge, lithium ions move inside the cell through the electrolyte, while electrons travel through the external circuit to do useful work. Recharging reverses the overall process.

**Anaya:** Two coordinated routes. The ions stay inside; the electrons take the working commute.

**Mira:** Cells are combined into a pack. A battery-management system monitors conditions, and thermal management helps keep operation within suitable temperatures. Different battery chemistries trade cost, energy density and other properties.

**Anaya:** So a battery pack is less a giant AA battery and more a closely supervised apartment complex.

**Mira:** And capacity and power are different. Kilowatt-hours measure energy stored; kilowatts measure the rate of energy transfer. Think tank size versus how quickly fuel can flow.

## 30. Charging: The last ten percent has questions

**Rowan:** Why does a huge charger number not guarantee a tiny charging time?

**Mira:** On an AC connection, the car's onboard charger converts alternating current into DC for the battery. With DC fast charging, that conversion happens outside the car.

**Mira:** Actual power depends on the charger, vehicle limits, battery temperature and state of charge. Charging commonly slows as the battery gets fuller.

**Rowan:** Like packing the last clothes into a suitcase, except the suitcase has sensors and refuses to negotiate.

**Mira:** Range also changes with speed, weather, terrain and heating or cooling demand. Charging infrastructure matters just as fuel infrastructure mattered to Bertha Benz.

**Rowan:** The pharmacy joke has become a charger-location app. History really does enjoy a callback.

## 31. Efficiency: The air also sends an invoice

**Malik:** If the electric motor is efficient, can we stop worrying about energy?

**Mira:** No. Any car needs energy to accelerate, climb, overcome tyre losses and push air aside. At otherwise similar conditions, aerodynamic drag force grows roughly with speed squared.

**Malik:** So driving faster makes the air increasingly uncooperative.

**Mira:** An electric car has no combustion tailpipe emissions, but manufacturing, battery production and electricity generation still have impacts. A life-cycle comparison includes those stages.

**Mira:** The electricity mix, vehicle size and use affect that comparison. Zero tailpipe emissions does not mean zero impact from everything involved in making and using the vehicle.

**Malik:** The exhaust pipe disappeared. The need to count honestly did not.

## 32. The software era: Assistance is not a chauffeur

**Nova:** Now the car can steer, brake and complain about lane markings. Has the driver become decorative?

**Mira:** Not with ordinary driver-assistance systems. Cameras, radar and other sensors can support features such as adaptive cruise control or lane assistance. Capabilities and limitations vary.

**Mira:** At Level Two automation, the system can control steering and speed together, but the driver must remain engaged and supervise. That is different from a system authorised to operate without a driver under defined conditions.

**Nova:** The label assistant is doing important work in that sentence.

**Mira:** Software also manages powertrains, diagnostics and many comfort functions. But a confident dashboard graphic is not proof that a car understands every situation.

**Nova:** We started by removing the horse. Let us not remove common sense as an optional extra.

## 33. Two centuries, one ongoing group project

**Milo:** Let me check the journey. Steam moved the carriage. Petrol made compact combustion practical. Production lines made cars easier to manufacture. Better chassis, tyres and brakes made them more usable.

**Nova:** Safety engineering protected people. Electronics refined control. Hybrids combined power sources, and modern batteries helped electric cars return to a much bigger stage.

**Milo:** Underneath all of it: store energy, convert it into torque, transmit it, find grip, and steer. Then slow down without ruining everyone's afternoon.

**Nova:** And every solution brings trade-offs: cost, weight, convenience, resources, pollution, safety and the space a car occupies. There is no single invention that settles every question.

**Milo:** So the history of cars is a centuries-long engineering group project.

**Nova:** Yes. With unusually expensive group members.

**Milo:** And the horse?

**Nova:** Still has four-wheel drive. Terrible Bluetooth.
