# Research and accuracy notes

Prepared 3 October 2026. SCRIPT.md is an educational comedy, not a diagnostic guide. Its conversations, examples and analogies are invented. The five labels do not all have equally strong or equally direct evidence. Source keys are editorial notes, not spoken dialogue.

## S1 — Spotlight effect

People in the original studies overestimated how noticeable their clothing and discussion contributions were. The explanation involves starting from one’s vivid personal experience and insufficiently adjusting to other perspectives. This supports “you may overestimate the audience,” not “almost nobody remembers anything embarrassing you ever do.” Our barista and pigeon jokes are illustrations, not experimental observations.

[Gilovich, Medvec & Savitsky (2000), original study and abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10707330/)

## S2 — Original Dunning–Kruger research

The original paper concerned performance and self-evaluation on particular tasks, including reasoning and grammar. Low-scoring participants overestimated their standing. A within-domain error is not a permanent label for a person’s intelligence. Nor does overestimation establish that beginners have higher absolute confidence than experts. The pizza example is invented.

[Kruger & Dunning (1999), original paper record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10626367/)

## S3 — Self-insight account

Further work examines whether weaker performers have difficulty recognising the quality of their performance, and tests explanations involving task knowledge and self-assessment. Use this to explain one account of the pattern, not to declare that anyone who disagrees with you lacks insight.

[Ehrlinger et al. (2008), further investigations](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2702783/)

## S4 — Statistical critique

A statistical model can reproduce the familiar Dunning–Kruger pattern without assuming a special psychological mechanism. This is a reason to mention measurement and statistical explanations alongside the self-insight account. Our script does not settle the debate. The popular “mountain of stupidity” learning-journey graph is not presented as a measured result from the original study.

[“A Statistical Explanation of the Dunning–Kruger Effect” (2022)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.840180/full)

## S5 — Bystander effect and context

A meta-analysis found an overall bystander effect with important moderators. Dangerous situations and the potential for support from others can reduce or reverse the usual negative pattern. Diffusion of responsibility and uncertainty about others’ reactions are useful explanatory ideas. The group-chat booking failure is an analogy, not an emergency study. Avoid the sweeping hook “twenty witnesses are worse than one.”

[Fischer et al. (2011), meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21534650/)

## S6 — Real-world helping

Research discussed by Levine and colleagues includes observations of real public conflicts where intervention was common. Distinguish an individual’s probability of helping from whether anyone in a crowd helps. These results concern sampled situations, not all emergencies. We avoid inventing a universal percentage or staging a dangerous event for a joke.

[Levine, Philpot & Kovalenko (2020), research review and implications](https://doi.org/10.1111/sipr.12063)

## S7 — Frequency illusion

Zwicky’s account connects the experience with selective attention and confirmation bias: things that become salient seem more frequent. His source is a conceptual linguistic account, not a controlled yellow-car experiment. The “Control F” comparison is our analogy. Increased noticing does not prove the real-world rate is unchanged; actual changes and shifts in attention can coexist.

[Arnold Zwicky (2006), “Why are we so illuded?”, Stanford](https://web.stanford.edu/~zwicky/LSA07illude.abst.pdf)

## S8 — Zeigarnik and Ovsiankina

The 2025 meta-analysis found no general recall advantage for unfinished tasks but did find a tendency to resume them. It distinguishes the Zeigarnik memory claim from the Ovsiankina resumption tendency. Motivation, involvement and situation can matter. We therefore separate curiosity about a cliffhanger, intrusive task-related thoughts, and measured memory performance. The browser-tab metaphor should not be read as a description of a literal brain mechanism.

[Ghibellini & Meier (2025), meta-analysis](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-025-05000-w)

## What to preserve when editing

- Spotlight: “can overestimate,” rather than a promise that nobody notices.
- Dunning–Kruger: domain-specific calibration, not “the least intelligent are the most confident.” Keep the statistical qualification.
- Bystanders: individual helping and crowd-level helping are different. Keep the contextual caveat.
- Frequency: attention can change without frequency changing; either or both can also change.
- Zeigarnik: keep the 2025 qualification. Do not recommend leaving work unfinished as a proven memory technique.
